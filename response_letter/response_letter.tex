\commentheading{Motivation and cross-tier heterogeneity}
\begin{quotedcomment}
The paper explains the coupling among handover, beamforming, and resource allocation in
HetNets. It would be beneficial to further strengthen the motivation by more clearly
articulating why this problem becomes fundamentally more unique and challenging in
heterogeneous networks, beyond differences in system architecture. In particular, additional
discussion on the new optimization challenges introduced by cross-tier heterogeneity could
further improve the clarity of the problem formulation.
\end{quotedcomment}
\begin{reviewedit}{blue}
\begin{authorresponse}
We thank the Reviewer for this helpful suggestion. We have expanded the
Introduction to explain how cross-tier heterogeneity complicates joint
optimization for reducing system transmit power. The revised discussion first clarifies the underlying coupling:
the serving BS and beam pair determine the link gain, which affects the
resources required to satisfy the traffic demands. It then connects cross-tier differences
to the resulting optimization tradeoffs.
Specifically, differences in RB bandwidths, per-RB transmit powers, and resource
budgets mean that the same traffic demand can incur different power costs and
may not be feasible at every candidate BS. Differences in carrier frequencies,
antenna configurations, and propagation conditions further affect beamforming
gains, training overhead, and channel dynamics, thereby affecting the service rate available after handover.
Moreover, handover redistributes traffic and resource usage, potentially changing
intra-tier co-channel interference and the resource demands of other links. These
interactions require association decisions to account jointly for beamforming
performance, resource availability, and the effects on other users when reducing
system transmit power under latency constraints.
\end{authorresponse}
\begin{manuscriptchanges}
The revised discussion reads:

{\emergencystretch=1em
\emph{The energy cost of serving traffic depends on the coupling between PHO, BF, and RA.
The serving BS and selected beam pair jointly determine the link gain and, consequently, the number of resource blocks (RBs) required to meet the traffic demand.
In HetNets, cross-tier differences in RB bandwidths, transmit powers, and resource budgets further complicate this coupling by changing the power cost and feasibility of serving a given traffic demand.
Meanwhile, differences in carrier frequencies, antenna configurations, and propagation conditions result in tier-dependent beamforming gains, beam-training overhead, and channel dynamics, thereby affecting post-handover service rates.
Moreover, handover redistributes traffic and resource usage across cells, potentially changing intra-tier co-channel interference and, consequently, the resource demands of other links.
This motivates energy-efficient {C}oordination of hand{O}ver, {B}eamforming and {R}esource {A}llocation (COBRA) in vehicular HetNets.}\par}
\end{manuscriptchanges}

\end{reviewedit}

\commentheading{Queueing delay and end-to-end latency}
\begin{quotedcomment}
The latency formulation is currently based on queue-length constraints. The paper could be
further strengthened by discussing the relationship between queueing delay and actual
end-to-end latency in a two-tier vehicular HetNet. Incorporating or discussing additional
latency components, such as handover interruption, beam training overhead, retransmissions,
and signaling delay, would improve the realism of the latency evaluation.
\end{quotedcomment}
\begin{reviewedit}{blue}
\begin{authorresponse}
We thank the Reviewer for this suggestion. Queueing delay at the serving BS
forms part of end-to-end latency, which also includes transmission, propagation,
and processing delays. In the considered two-tier HetNet, HO, BF, and RA
jointly affect the service rate available to each vehicle and, consequently,
its queueing delay. As clarified in Section II-C, $U$
measures threshold violations of the queueing-delay proxy
$\tau_v(n)=q_v(n)/\lambda_v$, not a directly measured packet-delay tail probability.

We have now incorporated a first-order HO interruption model. A vehicle
switching its serving BS receives no data service during the first
$L_{\rm HO}$ slots of the next frame. Its traffic arrivals continue to accumulate in
the queue during these slots, so the interruption affects the queue evolution
underlying $U$. Beam-training overhead is already accounted for through the
data-transmission fraction $1-\eta_{m,v}^{[x]}(i)$ in the receive rate. Thus,
both mechanisms affect the latency proxy through the available service rate.

The revised model distinguishes advance HO preparation from HO execution.
HO Preparation signaling is assumed to finish before the current frame ends without
occupying data-service resources, whereas HO execution incurs the modeled
data-service interruption. This work focuses on energy-efficient coordination
of PHO, BF, and RA under queue-length-based latency constraints. To keep the
modeling and analysis tractable, we assume successful handovers and do not
separately model failure recovery, retransmission delays, or other end-to-end
latency components. Consistent with this scope, the manuscript evaluates a
queueing-delay proxy rather than complete end-to-end latency.
\textcolor{blue}{GAP-HO now corrects the estimated RB demand in its RB-capacity
constraint to account for the slots lost to HO interruption. OTR-RA allocates
no RBs to vehicles during the interruption.}
\end{authorresponse}
\begin{manuscriptchanges}
The sentence added before the queueing-delay proxy definition in Section II-C reads:

\emph{Queueing delay is an important component of end-to-end latency and is
jointly affected by HO, BF, and RA.}

The HO assumptions added to Section II read:

\emph{After a PHO decision is made, the signaling exchanges required for HO
preparation are assumed to finish before the current frame ends, without
occupying data-service resources. A vehicle switching BS is assumed to experience
a data-service interruption of duration $\tau_{\rm HO}=L_{\rm HO}\Delta t$ by the 
HO execution at the start of the next frame,where $L_{\rm HO}$ is a nonnegative integer less than $N_{\rm HO}$. 
We assume successful HO execution for all PHO decisions. The macro control-plane
connection and periodic CSI probing remain available during this data-plane
HO interruption.}

The new indicator $\delta_v^{[x]}$ equals one when vehicle-$v$ changes its
serving BS between consecutive frames and zero otherwise. Equation~(3)
defines its data-service availability as
\[
s_v^{[x]}(i)=1-\delta_v^{[x]}\mathbf{1}\{i<L_{\rm HO}\}.
\]
Equation~(12) and constraint C4 of \textbf{P1} now enforce
\[
k_{m,v}^{[x]}(i)=u_{m,v}^{[x]}s_v^{[x]}(i)k_{m,v}^{[x]}(i).
\]
This makes the service rate zero during HO interruption in the existing
receive-rate and queue-update equations, Eqs.~(20) and (23). The latency
proxy and $U$ remain as defined in Section II-C.
\end{manuscriptchanges}

\commentheading{Interference-model consistency and validation}
\begin{quotedcomment}
The interference model is crude and inconsistent with the proposed algorithms. The
interference-link gain in Eq. (10) uses a Frobenius-norm average, justified by random transmit
and receive beam directions, and the resource-block overlap term in Eq. (16) assumes
independent and uniformly random allocation across base stations. Neither holds in the
proposed system, because every interfering micro base station runs the same deterministic
PET-BF, directing beams at its own vehicles, and the same deterministic OTR-RA, which places
resource blocks by efficiency rather than at random. Since the SINR in Eq. (17), and therefore
the entire power and latency story, rests on this approximation, please quantify how far these
surrogates deviate from directional interference, for example against a beam-aware interference
computation from Sionna RT, or justify why the bias is negligible at the reported loads.
\end{quotedcomment}
\begin{reviewedit}{blue}
\begin{authorresponse}
We thank the Reviewer for raising this concern. All revised system-level
simulation results in Figs.~5--8 have been recomputed using explicit RB assignments
and the actual transmit and receive beams. The receive rates computed in this way
are used to update the queues. The Frobenius-norm-based interfering-link gain predictions and the expected
number of overlapping RBs guide MEET-COBRA's decisions, but are not used to compute the
realized receive rates in simulations. We have also quantified the combined effect of
these two averaging approximations on receive-rate estimates in
Fig.~\ref{fig:response-interference-validation}.

Section II-A defines the directional interfering-link gain
$g_{{\rm I},m',v,v'}^{[x]}(i)$ in Eq.~(11), using the receive beam of
link $(m,v)$ and the transmit beam of link $(m',v')$.
Section IV-A separately introduces the beam-averaged surrogate
$\tilde{g}_{{\rm I},m',v}^{[x]}(i)$ in Eq.~(33), whose frame average
serves as the NN prediction target. For the complete, normalized DFT
codebooks, orthonormality gives
\[
\frac{1}{M_{\rm T}M_{\rm R}}
\sum_{\boldsymbol w_{\rm T}\in\mathcal C_{\rm T}}
\sum_{\boldsymbol w_{\rm R}\in\mathcal C_{\rm R}}
\left|\boldsymbol w_{\rm R}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol H\boldsymbol w_{\rm T}\right|^2
=\frac{\|\boldsymbol H\|_F^2}{M_{\rm T}M_{\rm R}}.
\]
This identity does not assume random beam selection by PET-BF. The
approximation is to use this uniform average to represent the gain under
the beams selected for data transmission.

For RB overlap issues, OTR-RA determines user priorities and their allocated RB counts, while
Section II-B specifies how these counts are mapped to RB indices. Specifically, each
micro BS assigns mutually disjoint RB sets to its associated vehicles uniformly at random,
independently of the other BSs. This mapping is compatible with
deterministic allocation of RB counts. Let $\mathcal S_{m,v}$ denote the
RBs assigned to vehicle-$v$ by BS-$m$ and $\mathcal S_{m'}$ those used by
an interfering BS-$m'$, with sizes $k_{m,v}$ and $k_{m'}$, respectively.
Suppressing the time indices, the conditional expected number of overlapping RBs is
\[
\mathbb E\!\left[|\mathcal S_{m,v}\cap\mathcal S_{m'}|
\mid k_{m,v},k_{m'}\right]
=\sum_{r=1}^{K_\mu}\frac{k_{m,v}}{K_\mu}\frac{k_{m'}}{K_\mu}
=\frac{k_{m,v}k_{m'}}{K_\mu}.
\]
Equation~(17) sums the contributions of the individual interfering links,
retaining their directional gains and averaging only over RB assignments.
Using this mean interference in the rate expression remains an approximation.

For online OTR-RA decisions, Eq.~(46) combines the desired-link gain
measured after PET-BF in each slot with the predicted frame-averaged
interfering-link gain $\Tilde{G}_{{\rm I},m',v}^{[x]}$ and the RB usage
estimates from GAP-HO. The gain prediction is generated in frame~$(x-1)$
and reused throughout frame-$x$, so instantaneous interfering-link gains
are not required. Section IV-E specifies that the macro BS forwards these
predictions and RB usage estimates to the serving BSs before the frame begins.

\textbf{Fixed-decision comparison of approximation error.}

Along each revised MEET-COBRA run, we compare receive rates calculated using
Eq.~(33) and the expected RB overlap with those obtained from directional
per-RB evaluation under identical
BS-vehicle associations, beam pairs, RB counts, and Sionna RT channels.
The latter calculation uses the actual beams and explicitly assigned RBs.
Both calculations account for beam-search overhead and HO interruption.
Using the channel-derived beam average in the diagnostic isolates the
combined averaging approximation from NN prediction error. The approximate
receive rates are recorded for comparison only and do not alter the queues.

We evaluate all arrival rates $\{1,3,\ldots,35\}$~Mbps using three seeds, with each run lasting $30$~s.
Figure~\ref{fig:response-interference-validation} shows the resulting
receive-rate underestimation at each load.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{figures/r2c1_interference_validation.pdf}
\captionof{figure}{Underestimation of the total receive rate of vehicles served
by micro BSs when using beam-averaged gains and expected RB overlaps, relative
to directional per-RB evaluation under identical decisions. Markers show means
over three seeds.}
\label{fig:response-interference-validation}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

For this comparison, we sum the receive rates over vehicles served by micro
BSs and average over slots. The plotted percentages are means of the per-seed
statistics in the same propagation scenario. Across the evaluated loads, the
two averaging approximations underestimate this total receive rate by
$6.5\%$--$19.5\%$ relative to directional per-RB evaluation under identical
decisions. These results indicate that the approximations provide an
empirically conservative estimate of the total receive rate under the
considered experimental settings.

\section{Reviewer 3}

\setcounter{reviewcomment}{0}
\generalheading{Summary}
\begin{quotedcomment}
This manuscript proposes MEET-COBRA, a framework for jointly coordinating handover,
beamforming, and resource allocation in a two-tier vehicular network, using machine learning
(LSTM) to predict channel gains and beam pairs ahead of time. The authors argue that solving
this joint problem exactly is computationally impractical and instead propose a two-timescale
approach: predictions drive the handover decision once per frame, while beamforming and
resource allocation adapt every slot based on those predictions and on real-time measurements.
The simulation setup combines SUMO mobility traces with Sionna ray-tracing channel data and
the evaluation includes both oracle bounds and per-module ablations that give a fairly complete
picture of where the reported gains come from. The proposed scheme shows a meaningful
reduction in transmit power and latency violations compared to a reactive baseline. I have a
number of concerns detailed below that should be addressed in a revision.
\end{quotedcomment}
\blankreply

\commentheading{ML computation and reporting overhead versus CSI overhead}
\begin{quotedcomment}
One of the paper's central motivations is reducing the overhead of channel state acquisition,
which is a reasonable goal. That said, in the proposed design, each vehicle needs to run three
separate ML prediction models locally every frame, one each for beam-pair selection,
desired-link gain, and interference-link gain, with all of it reported to the macro base station
each frame. This introduces its own computational and reporting overhead, and it isn't clear
from the paper how that overhead compares to the CSI overhead the framework is designed to
avoid in the first place. A brief discussion of this would strengthen the paper's claim of
achieving lower overhead with lightweight ML inference.
\end{quotedcomment}
\begin{reviewedit}{blue}
\begin{authorresponse}
We thank the Reviewer for this suggestion. We agree that the computational
and reporting costs should be quantified alongside the reduction in CSI
acquisition overhead. Section V-B now reports these costs and compares prediction
reporting with alternative CSI-reporting schemes.

For local computation, the three predictors together contain 2,127,912
parameters, require 8.51~MB of 32-bit floating-point (FP32) parameter storage,
and perform 4.20~MFLOPs of matrix computation per vehicle per frame, as
reported in Table~III. They retain recurrent states across frames and process
only the current-frame CSI, so per-frame computational complexity does not grow with
trajectory length. Using FP32 arithmetic, a batch size of one, and a single
thread on an AMD EPYC 7742 CPU, the three models have a combined inference
latency with a median of \textcolor{red}{5.38}~ms and a 95th percentile of \textcolor{red}{5.72}~ms, compared
with the 100~ms frame duration.

For CSI acquisition, the pilot-superposition procedure in Section IV-A
uses $N_{\rm P}=8$ probing rounds per frame, with all $M=4$ micro BSs
transmitting simultaneously in each round. Under the same receiver
observation model, exhaustively sweeping the $M_{\rm T}=32$ transmit beams
of each BS separately would require $MM_{\rm T}=128$ rounds.
Thus, pilot superposition reduces the number of frame-level CSI probing
rounds by a factor of $MM_{\rm T}/N_{\rm P}=16$.

For reporting, each vehicle sends, for each micro-BS link, the top-$M_{\rm P}$ beam-pair
indices in descending order of predicted probability and two FP32 gains. 
The full probability distribution is not uploaded. With $M=4$, $M_{\rm P}=5$, and
$M_{\rm T}M_{\rm R}=256$, the payload is
$M(8M_{\rm P}+2\times32)=416$ bits per vehicle per frame, or 4.16~kbps.
For comparison, two alternatives upload CSI instead of predictions.
At the same reporting interval and 32 bits per real CSI component,
full MIMO-CSI reporting requires $2MM_{\rm R}M_{\rm T}\times32=65{,}536$
bits, whereas current-frame superposed-pilot observations for centralized
inference require $2M_{\rm R}N_{\rm P}\times32=4{,}096$ bits per vehicle
per frame. In the latter case, the macro BS retains recurrent states, so
past observations need not be uploaded again. The two reference payloads
are 157.5 and 9.85 times the prediction-reporting payload, respectively.

Overall, MEET-COBRA reduces the overhead of frame-level CSI probing and uplink reporting at the cost of modest local computation per vehicle.

\end{authorresponse}
\begin{manuscriptchanges}
Section IV-A describes pilot superposition and stateful inference. Table~III
in Section V-B gives the parameter counts and matrix FLOPs of the three
predictors, followed by:

\emph{Table~III summarizes the NN parameter counts and matrix FLOPs for single-vehicle stateful inference using the current-frame CSI and recurrent states. The three models require 8.51~MB of 32-bit floating-point (FP32) parameter storage. With FP32 inference on an AMD EPYC 7742 CPU using one thread, their combined inference latency has a median of \textcolor{red}{5.38}~ms and a 95th percentile of \textcolor{red}{5.72}~ms.}

The resulting payload is $M(8M_{\rm P}+2\times32)=416$ bits per vehicle per frame (4.16~kbps). At the same reporting interval and 32-bit precision per real CSI component, this reduces the uplink payload by factors of 157.5 and 9.85 relative to full MIMO-CSI reporting and centralized inference using current-frame superposed-pilot observations, respectively.}
\end{manuscriptchanges}

\commentheading{Coordinated handover and beamforming baseline}
\begin{quotedcomment}
The comparison in Section V is against a reactive baseline, and that baseline is fairly weak on
all three fronts at once: handover triggered by signal-strength thresholds, beam selection
through random probing, and resource allocation under a worst-case interference assumption.
The per-module ablation study shown in the paper is a nice complement to this, since it shows
what each of the three proposed modules is individually contributing. It would also help to
compare against a coordinated handover and beamforming baseline from existing literature.
\end{quotedcomment}
\begin{reviewedit}{red}
\begin{authorresponse}
We thank the Reviewer for this suggestion. We have retained the module
ablations and added two baselines from the literature that coordinate HO
and BF, one based on reinforcement learning and the other on optimization.

\textbf{O-MAPPO-adapted}, adapted from Wang et al. [22], uses a MAPPO policy
to decide whether each vehicle should trigger HO. A mixed-integer optimizer
then selects the target BSs using RB demands, BS loads, and transmit power
costs. We adapt BF to our DFT codebooks, using exhaustive search upon HO to
a micro BS and local tracking otherwise. Unlike GAP-HO, it optimizes
associations only for vehicles whose HO triggers are active. Its decision
inputs are derived from true channel matrices, without accounting for the
additional CSI acquisition cost, giving it an information advantage over
MEET-COBRA.

\textbf{MTS-GS-HBF-adapted}, adapted from Heydarian et al. [23], uses
Gale--Shapley matching to update BS-vehicle associations every five frames.
We adapt the matching preferences and admission decisions to account for
predicted rates, BS loads, transmit power costs, HO costs, queue urgency,
and RB capacities. The original hybrid BF is replaced by probing the five
reported DFT candidates once per frame and retaining the strongest measured
pair. It uses the same prediction reports as MEET-COBRA, but coordinates
association and BF through preference matching and periodic beam selection,
rather than GAP-HO's system power objective and PET-BF's slot-level search.
Both baselines use OTR-RA, with their respective beam-search overheads and
the same $10$~ms HO interruption included in the evaluation.

Figs.~5--8 now compare all eight schemes at arrival rates of
$\{1,3,\ldots,35\}$~Mbps per vehicle, using three paired traffic seeds.
The table illustrates the comparison at $33$~Mbps and reports three-seed means.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\hline
Scheme & Mean power (W) & $U$ (\%) & $L_{99}$ (ms) \\
\hline
MEET-COBRA & 172.81 & 0.524 & 5.00 \\
O-MAPPO-adapted & 178.77 & 23.931 & 4570.90 \\
MTS-GS-HBF-adapted & 160.99 & 1.080 & 33.54 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

MEET-COBRA improves both power and latency performance over O-MAPPO-adapted
at this load. MTS-GS-HBF-adapted uses less power, but has a higher latency
constraint violation probability and a larger 99th-percentile queueing-delay
proxy $L_{99}$. These comparisons complement the module ablations by
evaluating MEET-COBRA against alternative HO and BF coordination methods
and showing their power and latency tradeoffs.
\end{authorresponse}
\begin{manuscriptchanges}
Section V-C introduces both baselines, explaining their core ideas,
adaptations, and differences from MEET-COBRA. Section V-D and Figs.~5--8
include their results across all evaluated loads and discuss the resulting
power and latency tradeoffs.
\end{manuscriptchanges}

\commentheading{Vehicle-speed distribution and mobility sensitivity}
\begin{quotedcomment}
Despite high-mobility vehicular scenarios being the paper's central motivation, it does not
report any information on vehicle speed anywhere in the manuscript. Speed is arguably one of
the main factors driving how quickly channel conditions change from one frame to the next, so
it directly affects how difficult the prediction problem actually is. The authors do state that
training and validation samples are drawn from non-overlapping time intervals of the same
simulated traffic, but the vehicle speed distribution within that dataset is never reported. It
would help to see the speed distribution used, and a result showing how performance of the
proposed framework changes across different mobility levels.
\end{quotedcomment}
