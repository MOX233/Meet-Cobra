\section*{Summary of Major Revisions}

\begin{reviewedit}{blue}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.45em,parsep=0pt]
\item \textbf{Modeling and technical consistency.}
Clarified the latency proxy and incorporated HO interruption with corresponding
algorithm updates. Distinguished the online interference approximations from
the directional interference calculations used in simulation and quantified
the resulting bias in receive-rate estimates.

\item \textbf{Expanded evaluation.}
Added two joint HO and BF baselines adapted from the literature, comparisons
using paired random seeds, and studies of vehicle-speed distributions,
performance by speed group, and gain-prediction errors.

\item \textbf{Implementation overhead and presentation.}
Quantified NN complexity, inference latency, and control-plane reporting
payloads. Explained parameter choices, clarified the extension beyond the
conference paper, expanded the discussion of related work, and added a notation
table.
\end{enumerate}
\end{reviewedit}

\begin{reviewedit}{blue}
\begin{authorresponse}
We thank the Editor for the opportunity to revise the manuscript and for
highlighting the main concerns. We have revised the manuscript in the
following three areas.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.55em,parsep=0pt]
\item \textbf{Modeling and technical consistency.}
We clarify the interpretation of the queueing-delay proxy and its violation
probability, incorporate HO interruption into queue evolution, and update
the PHO and RA algorithms accordingly. We also distinguish the interference
approximations used for online decisions from the directional interference
calculation with explicit RB assignments used in simulation. The combined
effect of these approximations on estimated receive rates is quantified
with associations, beam pairs, and RB counts held fixed.

\item \textbf{Expanded evaluation.}
To broaden the comparison, we introduce two literature-based joint HO and BF
baselines, O-MAPPO-adapted and MTS-GS-HBF-adapted. The former combines
reinforcement learning with target-BS optimization, whereas the latter applies
Gale--Shapley matching to BS--vehicle association and updates beamforming on
a shorter timescale than association. All schemes
are compared across traffic loads using paired random seeds.
We also report the vehicle-speed distributions
and evaluate prediction and latency performance by speed group. An
additional sensitivity study examines how gain-prediction errors affect
HO, beam probing, system transmit power, and latency performance.

\item \textbf{Implementation overhead and presentation.}
To quantify implementation overhead, we report NN parameter counts, parameter
storage, FLOPs, measured inference latency, and control-plane reporting
payloads. We explain the
algorithm parameter settings and clarify the contributions beyond the
conference version and related joint optimization studies. We also add a
notation table and revise terminology for consistency.
\end{enumerate}
\end{authorresponse}
\end{reviewedit}

\commentheading{Motivation and cross-tier heterogeneity}
\begin{quotedcomment}
The paper explains the coupling among handover, beamforming, and resource allocation in
HetNets. It would be beneficial to further strengthen the motivation by more clearly
articulating why this problem becomes fundamentally more unique and challenging in
heterogeneous networks, beyond differences in system architecture. In particular, additional
discussion on the new optimization challenges introduced by cross-tier heterogeneity could
further improve the clarity of the problem formulation.
\end{quotedcomment}
\begin{reviewedit}{blue}
\begin{authorresponse}
We thank the Reviewer for this helpful suggestion. We have expanded the
Introduction to explain how cross-tier heterogeneity complicates joint
optimization for reducing system transmit power. The revised discussion first clarifies the underlying coupling:
the serving BS and beam pair determine the link gain, which affects the
resources required to satisfy the traffic demands. It then connects cross-tier differences
to the resulting optimization tradeoffs.
Specifically, differences in RB bandwidths, per-RB transmit powers, and resource
budgets mean that the same traffic demand can incur different power costs and
may not be feasible at every candidate BS. Differences in carrier frequencies,
antenna configurations, and propagation conditions further affect beamforming
gains, training overhead, and channel dynamics, thereby affecting the service rate available after handover.
Moreover, handover redistributes traffic and resource usage, potentially changing
intra-tier co-channel interference and the resource demands of other links. These
interactions require association decisions to account jointly for beamforming
performance, resource availability, and the effects on other users when reducing
system transmit power under latency constraints.
\end{authorresponse}
\begin{manuscriptchanges}
The revised discussion reads:

{\emergencystretch=1em
\emph{The energy cost of serving traffic depends on the coupling between PHO, BF, and RA.
The serving BS and selected beam pair jointly determine the link gain and, consequently, the number of resource blocks (RBs) required to meet the traffic demand.
In HetNets, cross-tier differences in RB bandwidths, transmit powers, and resource budgets further complicate this coupling by changing the power cost and feasibility of serving a given traffic demand.
Meanwhile, differences in carrier frequencies, antenna configurations, and propagation conditions result in tier-dependent beamforming gains, beam-training overhead, and channel dynamics, thereby affecting post-handover service rates.
Moreover, handover redistributes traffic and resource usage across cells, potentially changing intra-tier co-channel interference and, consequently, the resource demands of other links.
This motivates energy-efficient {C}oordination of hand{O}ver, {B}eamforming and {R}esource {A}llocation (COBRA) in vehicular HetNets.}\par}
\end{manuscriptchanges}

\commentheading{Queueing delay and end-to-end latency}
\begin{quotedcomment}
The latency formulation is currently based on queue-length constraints. The paper could be
further strengthened by discussing the relationship between queueing delay and actual
end-to-end latency in a two-tier vehicular HetNet. Incorporating or discussing additional
latency components, such as handover interruption, beam training overhead, retransmissions,
and signaling delay, would improve the realism of the latency evaluation.
\end{quotedcomment}
\begin{reviewedit}{blue}
\begin{authorresponse}
We thank the Reviewer for this suggestion. Queueing delay at the serving BS
forms part of end-to-end latency, which also includes transmission, propagation,
and processing delays. In the considered two-tier HetNet, HO, BF, and RA
jointly affect the service rate available to each vehicle and, consequently,
its queueing delay. As clarified in Section II-C, $U$
measures threshold violations of the queueing-delay proxy
$\tau_v(n)=q_v(n)/\lambda_v$, not a directly measured packet-delay tail probability.

We have now incorporated a first-order HO interruption model. A vehicle
switching its serving BS receives no data service during the first
$L_{\rm HO}$ slots of the next frame. Its traffic arrivals continue to accumulate in
the queue during these slots, so the interruption affects the queue evolution
underlying $U$. Beam-training overhead is already accounted for through the
data-transmission fraction $1-\eta_{m,v}^{[x]}(i)$ in the receive rate. Thus,
both mechanisms affect the latency proxy through the available service rate.

The revised model distinguishes advance HO preparation from HO execution.
HO preparation signaling is assumed to finish before the current frame ends without
occupying data-service resources, whereas HO execution incurs the modeled
data-service interruption. This work focuses on energy-efficient coordination
of PHO, BF, and RA under queue-length-based latency constraints. To keep the
modeling and analysis tractable, we assume successful handovers and do not
separately model failure recovery, retransmission delays, or other end-to-end
latency components. Consistent with this scope, the manuscript evaluates a
queueing-delay proxy rather than complete end-to-end latency.
\textcolor{blue}{GAP-HO now corrects the estimated RB demand in its RB-capacity
constraint to account for the slots lost to HO interruption. OTR-RA allocates
no RBs to vehicles during the interruption.}
\end{authorresponse}
\begin{manuscriptchanges}
The sentence added before the queueing-delay proxy definition in Section II-C reads:

\emph{Queueing delay is an important component of end-to-end latency and is
jointly affected by HO, BF, and RA.}

The HO assumptions added to Section II read:

\emph{After a PHO decision is made, the signaling exchanges required for HO
preparation are assumed to finish before the current frame ends, without
occupying data-service resources. A vehicle switching BS is assumed to experience
a data-service interruption of duration $\tau_{\rm HO}=L_{\rm HO}\Delta t$ caused by
HO execution at the start of the next frame, where $L_{\rm HO}$ is a nonnegative integer less than $N_{\rm HO}$.
We assume successful HO execution for all PHO decisions. The macro control-plane
connection and periodic CSI probing remain available during this data-plane
HO interruption.}

The new indicator $\delta_v^{[x]}$ equals one when vehicle-$v$ changes its
serving BS between consecutive frames and zero otherwise. Equation~(3)
defines its data-service availability as
\[
s_v^{[x]}(i)=1-\delta_v^{[x]}\mathbf{1}\{i<L_{\rm HO}\}.
\]
Equation~(12) and constraint C4 of \textbf{P1} now enforce
\[
k_{m,v}^{[x]}(i)=u_{m,v}^{[x]}s_v^{[x]}(i)k_{m,v}^{[x]}(i).
\]
This makes the service rate zero during HO interruption in the existing
receive-rate and queue-update equations, Eqs.~(20) and (23). The latency
proxy and $U$ remain as defined in Section II-C.
\end{manuscriptchanges}

\commentheading{Propagation of prediction errors}
\begin{quotedcomment}
The paper presents promising prediction accuracy results. It would be valuable to further
analyze how prediction errors propagate to higher-layer decisions, particularly proactive
handover performance. Additional experiments quantifying the impact of prediction errors on
HO reliability, association accuracy, or latency performance would provide deeper insight
into the robustness of the proposed framework.
\end{quotedcomment}
\begin{reviewedit}{blue}
\begin{authorresponse}
We thank the Reviewer for this suggestion. We have added an explanation of
prediction error propagation in Section IV-E and a sensitivity study in
Section V-E. The study quantifies the influence of prediction errors on
HO, beam probing, system transmit power and latency performance.

Gain-prediction errors change the RB demand estimates in Eq.~(40), and hence
the estimated power costs and RB requirements used by GAP-HO to select
BS--vehicle associations. PET-BF depends on the predicted beam-pair list and
search order, while its early-termination threshold depends on the predicted
desired-link gain. OTR-RA uses measured desired-link gains, but errors in the
predicted interfering-link gains and estimated BS RB usages affect its
RB-efficiency estimates and allocation decisions. These effects propagate
to the queue evolution and violation probability $U$ through the resulting
receive rates.

To quantify the effect of gain-prediction errors, we add independent
zero-mean Gaussian noise in dB, with standard deviation
$\sigma\in\{0,1,\ldots,10\}$~dB, to either the desired-link or interfering-link
gain predictions, changing only one gain type at a time. The trained models,
beam-pair candidates, and algorithm parameters remain fixed. For each traffic load and random seed, 
the actual channels and traffic arrivals are identical
across noise settings. We rerun the MEET-COBRA framework at
$9$, $19$, $29$, and $35$~Mbps using three paired seeds, with each run covering $30$~s.
The case $\sigma=0$ retains the original NN prediction errors.

At $29$~Mbps, adding desired-link gain noise with $\sigma=5$~dB increases
the mean number of HOs per frame from $3.70$ to $20.01$ and the mean
number of probes per beam search from $1.06$ to $1.95$. The latter statistic
is averaged over the beam searches actually performed, excluding macro-BS
associations and HO-interrupted slots. Additional HOs and beam probes reduce the time available for data transmission.
At this operating point, $U$ increases from $0.19\%$ to $0.29\%$.
These results show the influence of gain-prediction errors on MEET-COBRA, while
MEET-COBRA still maintains a low violation probability at this load.

Figure~10 shows that $U$ changes little at $9$ and $19$~Mbps for
$\sigma\leq5$~dB. At $35$~Mbps, however, adding noise with $\sigma=5$~dB
raises $U$ from $1.15\%$ to $3.84\%$ for desired-link gain errors and
$3.13\%$ for interfering-link gain errors. Figure~11 reports the
corresponding power results. Larger errors generally increase power at
lower loads, whereas power remains close to the full-load level at
$35$~Mbps as $U$ increases. Thus, the experiments demonstrate tolerance
to moderate additional gain-prediction errors at light and medium loads.
\end{authorresponse}
\begin{manuscriptchanges}
Section IV-E adds a paragraph tracing prediction errors through GAP-HO,
PET-BF, and OTR-RA to the receive rates, queue evolution, and $U$.
The new Section V-E, \emph{Impact of Gain-Prediction Errors}, describes the
noise-injection experiment and presents $U$ and average system transmit
power in Figs.~10 and 11, respectively. It also reports the changes in HO
counts and probes per beam search and explains their effect on the time
available for data transmission. The Conclusion summarizes the observed
error tolerance at light and medium loads.
\end{manuscriptchanges}

\setcounter{reviewcomment}{0}
\generalheading{Overall Assessment and Strengths}
\begin{quotedcomment}
The joint treatment of proactive handover, beamforming, and resource allocation is a real and
underexplored gap, and using the per-link resource-block demand as the common currency that
links prediction, association, and scheduling is clean and well motivated. The evaluation
platform is credible, since combining SUMO traces with Sionna RT ray tracing is more realistic
than the stochastic channel models most comparable papers use. The algorithmic engineering is
careful, with per-module complexity analysis, a Shmoys--Tardos approximation for the assignment
step with a stated two-factor capacity bound, and a reasonable ablation and oracle design.

\textbf{1) NN computational complexity and inference latency.}

Online inference processes the current-frame CSI and retains recurrent

trajectory length. With FP32 inference on an AMD EPYC 7742 CPU using one thread
and batch size one, the three models have a combined inference latency of
\textcolor{blue}{5.38}~ms at the median and \textcolor{blue}{5.72}~ms at the 95th percentile, corresponding to

Timing includes tensor conversion, the three forward passes, state retention,
top-5 index selection, and gain denormalization. To measure NN inference
separately, the timed interval starts with preprocessed CSI and ends with
the prediction outputs, excluding CSI acquisition, input preprocessing, and
report transmission. The reporting payload is quantified below.

\textbf{2) Control-plane reporting budget.}

For each micro-BS link, the vehicle reports the top-$M_{\rm P}$ beam-pair
indices in descending order of predicted probability and two FP32 gains,
one for the desired link and one for the interfering link. The full
probability distribution is not uploaded. With $M=4$, $M_{\rm P}=5$, and
$M_{\rm T}M_{\rm R}=256$, each index requires 8 bits, giving
$4(5\times8+2\times32)=416$ bits per vehicle per frame, or 4.16~kbps for
100~ms frames.

To assess the reporting budget savings, we compare this payload with two alternative
schemes that upload CSI instead of prediction results, not in addition to them.
At the same reporting interval and 32 bits per real CSI component, uploading
all complex MIMO channel matrices requires
$2MM_{\rm R}M_{\rm T}\times32=65{,}536$ bits per vehicle per frame
(655.36~kbps). Uploading

\textbf{3) HO interruption model.}

The revised model distinguishes advance HO preparation from HO execution. The signaling exchanges required for advance HO preparation are assumed to be completed before the end of the current frame without consuming data-service resources.
When a vehicle changes its serving BS, HO execution at the start of the next frame interrupts data service for $\tau_{\rm HO}=L_{\rm HO}\Delta t$ seconds, where $0\leq L_{\rm HO}<N_{\rm HO}$. The control-plane connection to the macro BS and periodic CSI probing remain available throughout this interruption.

For frame-level PHO, the revised GAP-HO accounts for the shorter service period after
switching BS by applying the HO-aware correction factor $c_{m,v}^{[x+1]}$ to the estimated RB demand in the
RB-capacity constraint of \textbf{P2}. This factor is equal to one if the serving BS
does not change and $N_{\rm HO}/(N_{\rm HO}-L_{\rm HO})$ if it changes.
